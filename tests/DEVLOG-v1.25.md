# v1.25 — FIRST SHIFT

Every legend starts somewhere. Usually flat on their back in the grass, wondering why the bugs are wearing THEM.

**THE FIRST SHIFT:** Start a brand-new save slot and the game walks you through your first day on the crew — six little jobs, in order, on a slim gold-cornered card at the bottom of the screen. Squash your first bug. Wear a 10 KG hat. Build a wall out of the evidence. Buy something at the Bug Bounty. Bank your carcasses at the Bug Bank. Step into any Arcade cabinet. Each job done pays your Banzai Buddy a little XP and deals the next card. When a job is a PLACE, a pale arrow rides the screen with you and points the way — through the Mall hut, down the Arcade row — until you're close enough to see the spot marked for yourself.

No buddy yet when the XP starts flowing? It isn't lost. The game holds it in trust and hands over every last point the moment you adopt — tuition, paid in full.

The Shift minds its manners. It only ever reports for duty on a genuinely fresh slot — your old saves will never see it, not once. It keeps out of the way in the Mall's shop panels, shares everything between both players in local co-op, and if you'd rather find your own way, the pause menu carries a SKIP FIRST SHIFT row for exactly as long as the Shift is running. One tap and it clocks out forever, no hard feelings, no penalty.

Finish the last job and the card flips one final time: FIRST SHIFT COMPLETE. Then it never nags you again. You're on the crew now. The bugs, regrettably, have been informed.

**COMFORT SETTINGS:** The SETTINGS screen grows four new rows at the top, because a crew that works this loud needs a volume knob. MUSIC VOLUME and SOUND FX VOLUME — ten honest blocks each, from a whisper to the full brass band. A MUTE ALL switch, for 11 pm heroics: the baby is asleep, the bugs are not. And a SCREEN SHAKE switch that stills the screen when the big ones go off. Turning the SFX knob clicks at the new level so you can hear your decision, and everything you set is kept — next session opens at your volume, not ours.

The shake itself got its wiring finished along the way: the big hits — the PM explosion, a buddy's TIMBER! — have always wound up for a proper camera thump, and now the camera finally answers. Prefer a steady screen while the evidence flies? One switch turns the thump off everywhere at once.

Your old saves know nothing about any of this, which is exactly how they like it: they load with the volume up and the shake on, same as they always were.

**MEDAL TIMES:** The Arcade has started keeping score properly. Every SUPER BUDDY CART track and every SUPER BUDDY RUN level now carries three medal times — BRONZE, SILVER, GOLD — and your best time on each is remembered for good. Beat a medal time and the finish panel says so in the medal's own colour; your medals ride in the waiting room over the cup you're about to run, in the standings after every race, and on the brand-new Run level select, where returning runners can finally pick any unlocked level and go back for the gold they left behind. The times were not plucked from the air: the Cart's were set off the slowest rival's own lap pace, track by track, so bronze means you ran with the pack and gold means you outran everyone on it. The Run's were set off the runner's own legs — bronze is a finish with time to fight, gold is a finish at nearly full tilt. Zen runs, as ever, set no records and take no medals home. Eddie runs count. The medal book covers the first sixteen levels and all eight tracks — beyond that, you're running for the love of it.

**CONTEXTUAL HUD CONTROLS:** The game's instructions have learned to look at your hands. Every prompt in the game — the Mall door, the shop counters, the Arcade cabinets, the WAR! briefing map, the pause menu, all of it — now names the control you are actually holding. On a keyboard it names your keys, honouring your own bindings. On a gamepad it speaks in buttons: A, B, X, Y, START. On a phone it points at the on-screen buttons themselves, FIRE and USE and WALL, because on a phone the prompts ARE the buttons. And it keeps up live: put the keyboard down and pick up a pad mid-session and the very next hint has already changed its mind. WAR! has stopped telling mouse players to TAP things — they get CLICK, which is what their hand was doing all along — and in split-screen co-op, each half of the screen talks to its own player: Player Two's half speaks gamepad even while Player One's half is mid-keyboard. The save slots finally introduce themselves, too. No more reading somebody else's instructions.

**TROPHY CASE:** The Bug Mall has a new stop, and for once nobody there is selling anything. On the south wall, just past the Banzai World gate, stands a glass TROPHY CASE with your name on it — or it will have, once you've earned anything. Inside, on three shelves, sit the plaques for everything this save slot has ever done. The Cabinets shelf keeps the conquests: CASTLE beaten and your best score, Zed defeated in STORY MODE, WAR! regions conquered, Grand Prix cups won, Tank Rescue's deepest level, Entrances escaped and the Subfloor's lowest floor. The middle shelf holds your medal counts — Cart golds, silvers and bronzes beside Run's — and the Walk of Fame's house records: heaviest hat, most bugs squashed, best Bug Bowl game, longest run. The bottom shelf is the crew: your First Shift card, your buddy by name, and how the training is coming along.

Nothing in the case is new bookkeeping. The game was already writing all of it down — the case just finally puts it behind glass where you can admire it. Every plaque starts as an honest NOT YET with a note underneath saying exactly what earns it, so a fresh save's case isn't empty — it's a to-do list with better lighting. Medals and plaques are struck in gold the moment they're earned; the rest wait as hollow rings. And because the Walk of Fame belongs to the whole house, its records are shared by every slot, while the cabinet plaques are each slot's own. Zen victories, as ever, are their own reward and appear nowhere. Glory requires paperwork.

**HAT TOPPERS:** The carcass hat has always been a trophy you wear by accident. Now it gets one on purpose. Eight HAT TOPPERS are hidden in the game's cabinets and corners: a CROWN for beating CASTLE, a GENERAL CAP for finishing the WAR!, a CHECKER FLAG for taking a Grand Prix cup, a STORY MEDAL for putting Zed down, a BOWL PIN for a 200 game at the lanes, a GOLD STAR for your first gold medal, an ANVIL for the buddy who has stacked 160 kilos of evidence, and a HARD HAT for finishing your First Shift. Earn one and it is yours for good — the game checks the very same records the Trophy Case reads, so the plaque and the topper can never disagree about what you've done. Fit them at BANZAI BUDDIES: FIRE on the HAT TOPPER row cycles through everything you've won. A topper weighs nothing, spends nowhere and slows nothing down. It simply sits on top of the stack, riding the sway, looking extremely earned. Even on a bare head. Even — especially — on a stack of Eddies.

**WEEKLY CHALLENGE:** The Mall's south wall has a new board at the west end, and it isn't selling anything either. Every Monday the CHALLENGE BOARD pins up one fresh challenge — SQUASH 50 BUGS. WEAR A HAT OF 100 KG. BANK 200 KG. CONQUER A WAR! REGION. EARN A NEW MEDAL. FINISH A STORY QUEST. — and it is the same challenge for everybody, everywhere, because it is worked out from the week itself rather than dealt out by chance. The board runs on your own machine's clock; it is the only clock the game has ever had. Finish the week's work and your buddy banks 50 XP the moment it lands — no trip back to the board, no form in triplicate. The pin on the card turns green, the ledger in the board's book counts the finish, and the pause menu keeps your standing all week: WEEKLY: MEDAL HUNT — 1/2. Miss a week and precisely nothing happens. No streak to break, no fine, no guilt: last week's card hangs in the corner marked DONE or MISSED, and Monday pins up a new one either way. Co-op crews share the one challenge between them, and every buddy on the crew gets paid. Zen weeks count too — the board keeps no records, only finishes.

**CASTLE SHUFFLE:** The castle has been solved. You know where the key sleeps, which crypt bat is decorative, and exactly how smug the ogre in the dungeon is about it. So the castle has started cheating. Escape it once — properly, through the gate, the way the plaque in the Trophy Case counts it — and the CASTLE cabinet grows a new row: PLAY SHUFFLE. The shuffle changes nothing you can see on a map: same twenty rooms, same doors, same draught. What it re-deals is the CONTENTS. The thirteen treasures, the sword, the tonics and all fifteen monsters are shuffled from a seed into new rooms and new corners, and the key — the key could be anywhere. Behind a demon. In the chapel. Guarded by snakes in a room you used to stroll through. The castle makes three promises and keeps all of them: the courtyard stays empty and safe, the sword and the key never share a room, and nothing ever lands inside the stonework, so every deal can be beaten — the gate still only wants the key, and Escape still walks you out whenever you've had enough, key or no key. Every shuffle run deals the next seed in line, so the castle you solved last Tuesday is not the castle waiting tonight. And it all counts: a shuffle escape feeds the same BEST on the same plaque and earns the same crown, because the score was never about the furniture. It was always about getting out.

**SIZE STRETCH:** The giants have not been cancelled. They have been given a longer road. Everything about getting stronger still scales the way it always did — the crowds keep thickening, the far country keeps growing teeth, nothing anywhere has been capped — but SIZE itself now takes the scenic route: bugs grow at a third of the old pace, so the doorstep swarm at high THREAT averages a merely unreasonable 34 kilos instead of a wall-to-wall 52, and you can still walk around, aim, and have a day. The truly enormous ones are all still out there, arriving exactly as promised. They just need three times the journey to reach you, which means when a bug the size of a house finally does come over the horizon, it has EARNED the horizon. Same destination. Longer road. The ridiculous is not reduced; it is rescheduled.

**SKIRMISH FIX:** A confession from the war room. The SKIRMISH button on the WAR! world map only ever answered to two callers: the letter S on a keyboard, and a fingertip. If you command your wars from a gamepad — stick in hand, every region invaded by stick and button — the setup panel simply never opened. The button sat there bottom-right, glowing politely, while A and B launched region after region of a campaign you had already beaten. Skirmish has stood ready since Boot Camp; a whole class of commanders just had no door to it. Now the map's selection walks one stop further: past the eighth region it lands on SKIRMISH itself, lit gold like a picked node, and A opens the panel the way A opens everything. The panel's manners are fixed too — its hint always promised that B backs out, and B always lied, confirming whatever row it was pointed at, LAUNCH included. B backs out now, as advertised. The keyboard keeps its S, the mouse keeps its click, the finger keeps its tap. The war is for everybody again.

**NAMED ELITES:** Most bugs are weather. A few are *news*. Hanging on the Mall's west wall — the one wall with nothing on it — the BOUNTY BOARD posts a single name at a time: NULLPOINTER, DEVOURER OF STACKS. SEGFAULT, THE UNHANDLED EXCEPTION. HEISENBUG, GONE WHEN YOU LOOK AT IT. Twelve names in the book, chalked up one after another, and every one of them belongs to a bug out there that grew three times too big for its boots and got ideas. The named ones crawl out when they please — seldom near home, more often the further out you wander, which is the board's way of paying for expeditions. You'll know yours by the amber name riding over it and the amber arrow that points the way while it's loose; the board keeps the last place it was seen, so a hunter always has somewhere to start. Bring it down and there is no form to file and no trip back: the claim settles where it falls. The killer banks the thing's whole weight a second time — the carcass on your hat, and its weight again in the Bug Bank — and every buddy on the crew earns 25 XP for the lesson. Then the board chalks up the next name and the wall goes quiet for a while. And if the thing slips away instead — outruns you, outlasts you, watches you duck into the Mall from a safe distance — the contract stands. It is still out there. It will crawl out again. Zen hunters welcome: the board keeps no records, only names, and a name is a name in any mode.

**HAT PORTRAIT BOOTH:** The Bug Mall has eight stops and not one of them would take your picture. That has been remedied — not with a ninth stop, because the walls are full and the Banzai Buddies counter objected to the competition, but with a curtain at the back of the counter itself. PHOTO BOOTH, last row of the buddy menu, free as everything at that counter is free. Step in and the frame is already lit: you, your buddy, and the hat — the whole absurd tower of it, swaying gently under the marquee bulbs. Six backdrops hang in the booth, all stolen from somewhere you have bled: PLAZA DAWN. FROZEN CACHE. KERNEL BADLANDS. CABLE JUNGLE. THE BUG MALL itself, for the traditionalists, and the WALK OF FAME, for standing in front of the records while wearing one. Pose with the hat AS WORN, or turn the dial to BARE and sit bare-headed and proud — the dial is a pose, not a decision; your real hat is never touched, never taxed, never so much as breathed on.

FIRE on SNAP PORTRAIT and the flash does two things at once. The pose freezes — the backdrop, every carcass in the stack exactly as stacked, the topper exactly as worn, the weight lettered on the plate in kilos — and hangs under glass at the back of the booth, where it stays. Bank the hat, spend the hat, lose the hat to a doorway in the Depths: the portrait keeps it. That is the day you wore it, and the glass does not care what you did next. And because a keepsake you cannot take with you is only half a keepsake, the same flash hands you the picture itself — a proper portrait file, saved the way the score card is saved, share sheet and all. Snap as often as you like; the booth charges nothing, because the hat was never the booth's to tax. The newest portrait hangs over the last one, and the files are the archive. Zen crews sit for portraits too — a picture is not a record, and the Trophy Case, which keeps only records, politely looks the other way. Eddie sits as himself, as always. Say 'BUGS!'

**BUG BOWL LEAGUE:** The lanes have regulars now. Three of them, and they were here before you: KINGPIN, who bowls like the pins owe him money; SPARE CHANGE, who has never met a spare she wouldn't pick up; and GUTTER BUG, who is just happy to be included and bowls accordingly. The league runs six nights a season, and the fixture list is already written — every rival's score for every night of the season was chalked up before the season started, because their scores are worked out from the season itself and not dealt out by chance. Walk up to the lanes and the card tells you tonight's numbers before you lift a single carcass: TARGET: 154. TIES GO TO YOU. Ties always go to you. It is the house's one act of mercy, and Gutter Bug is not bitter about it. There is no signing up and no separate league night to schedule — every real game you bowl IS the next league night, in order, whether you meant it to be or not, and the standings keep themselves in cumulative pins on the wall beside the lane where they belong. The house keeps your book now too, per bowler: games, best game, total pins, and every strike, counted honestly — a strike is ten pins off a full rack, not ten pins however they came. Finish the season on top and your buddy banks 50 XP and the trophy goes on the wall; the next season's card is already chalked up underneath. Zen games are practice, and the book pretends they never happened, which is more than can be said for some of Gutter Bug's real ones.

**SUPER BUDDY RUN PLAYTEST:** Somebody finally sat down and played the running game like they meant it, and the running game had two confessions to make. First: the speed limit was a polite fiction. The runner's top speed was tuned and retuned — 100 up the hill, 124 flat out — but the maths underneath was adding more go than it took away, every single step, for as long as you held the button. A held run didn't level off at the cap; it climbed past it, quietly, forever — 300, 400, 600 units a second and still rising, jumps sailing clean over whole postal districts. No wonder it always felt too fast no matter where the dial was set. The cap is a cap now: a push at full speed just holds it there, and a knockback's extra shove still bleeds off the way it always meant to. Second confession: the checkpoint was jumping the gun. The little flag at the level's midpoint is only supposed to count once you've run to it — but it was arming itself a few steps out of the spawn gate, so any pit you fell into, anywhere in the first half, dropped you back into the world at the MIDDLE of the level, past everything you'd been sent there to run. It even banked medal times for courses nobody ran — a dive into the first hole on level 1 was worth a gold. The post stands honest now: fall before it and you start at the start, fall after it and you start at the post, and the flag only turns green when you've actually passed it. The medals didn't move. They were costed for the full course at the real top speed, and now — for the first time — that's the race everybody is actually running.

**WAR! PAD:** Two confessions from the war room's gamepad desk, both reported by a commander who fights his wars stick in hand. First: the capture that never landed. Selecting a building was supposed to hand your pad to the command bar — cursor captured, stick walking the menu, B to peel out — and on the test bench it did, every time. In a player's hands it kept missing. The bench pressed A with the stick politely released; a player presses A while still pushing toward the building, and the still-held stick stepped the menu once for free the moment the capture opened, so the lit button was never the button you wanted. And a player steering by D-pad — the other half of every pad ever made — couldn't aim the cursor at all: the pad walked the camera, never the finger, so no building was ever truly pointed at. Both doors are open now. The capture opens on the first option, exactly as briefed, however you arrived; and the D-pad wakes, flies, selects and walks the menu shoulder to shoulder with the stick. Second confession: the wandering cursor. It could end a push pinned against the edge of the screen and then stay pinned, offset, in every direction, like a picture hung crooked in a house that had settled. The camera was being shoved by the same stick that flew the cursor, at a different speed, and the difference had nowhere to go but into the offset. That machinery is gone. The cursor is the camera now: it sits at the centre of the field, always, and both sticks simply fly it. Push to the very corner of the map and the view does the honest thing — it scrolls past the fence and shows you the dark beyond it, compensated, while your cursor stays dead centre on the corner post. There is no crooked picture anymore. There is only the middle of the war, which is wherever you're pointing.

**MALL ENLARGEMENT:** The Bug Mall has been measured for new walls, and the walls lost. The whole room has stretched — a hundred paces wider on each side, forty deeper front and back — and every stop has room to breathe at last. The four counters along the north wall stand at parade-ground spacing now. The Trophy Case and the Challenge Board bookend a south wall that finally fits them, the Banzai gate has elbow room, and the Bounty Board has a stretch of west wall it no longer has to share with two other discs. And the Photo Booth — which opened as a curtain at the back of the Banzai Buddies counter because there was nowhere else to put it — has a home of its own: a curtained island standing west-centre, marquee sign and all, the Mall's NINTH stop. Same booth, same free snaps, same portrait under glass; it just answers its own door now, and the buddy counter's menu is back to buddies. While the builders were in, the painters fixed the lettering. The shop counter at the west end is THE COUNTER now — plain, honest, and no longer sharing a name with the elite board on the west wall (the old BUG BOUNTY hut is history, and the help page says so, as history). The signs count properly at last: NINE FINE STOPS, inside and out. And the help book itself has grown a third page, because the Mall's page had quietly run off the bottom of its own panel — the BIG HATS and WALLS paragraphs were down there in the dark. Everything sits on a page that fits now, the booth's new address included: THE PHOTO BOOTH STANDS ALONE ON THE MALL'S WEST SIDE.

**DEEPER DOORS:** The cozy hotel of doors has a back corridor, and it has been there the whole time. Escape the ENTRANCES hotel once — out the exit door, the way Chapter 1 counts it — and the cabinet grows a third row: DEEPER DOORS. Behind it waits a second hotel on the same street: forty rooms deep, painted in the deep teal-green of the hour after bedtime, every door, drawer, key and hiding bush re-dealt from a fresh shuffle, so the hotel you memorised is not the hotel waiting down the corridor. The residents keep their Chapter 1 manners and their Chapter 1 jobs — the Zip Bug still owns its hallway, the Inspector still makes his rounds in the middle stretch, the Typo still whispers from the dark rooms — and the house rules have not changed by a single letter: bonks, kazoos, a nap on the Mall carpet at worst. Nothing down here ends anybody. It is the same kind hotel, one corridor deeper.

You do not go down alone. LUMEN comes with you — a lantern buddy, a round amber firefly with more enthusiasm than altitude, who takes up station at your shoulder the moment you step through and keeps it the whole way down. In the lit rooms he is company. In the dark rooms — and the corridor keeps Chapter 1's habit of a dark room every fifth door — he is the difference: his glow stretches your little circle of light to nearly double, so the whispering shapes have to introduce themselves from much further away. And then there are the doors that were built for two. A DUET DOOR wears its name on a brass plate and keeps a brass floor plate on either side of its row, and no key in the building fits it, because keys were never the point. Stand on one plate yourself and Lumen — who has been waiting his whole life for exactly this — takes the other. Hold still together for one shared breath and the door opens the way doors in this hotel always wanted to: for two friends at once. Step off early and it simply waits, unbothered; there is no timer, no penalty, no score for style. The corridor keeps its own book, too — DEEPER BEST on the run card, and a count of your escapes — filed separately from Chapter 1's, which sits exactly where you left it, records, library and all. The library, for the record, stayed upstairs. Forty rooms is quite deep enough without carrying the books.

**DAY, NIGHT, AND WEATHER:** The sky over the Fields has started keeping hours. It always knew what time it was — your device has been telling it all along, the same clock that deals the Weekly Challenge — it just never bothered to dress for it. Now it does. The morning comes up amber at half past four and takes its time about it; by half past six the world is plain daylight and stays that way until early evening, when the amber returns the other way and the blue comes down behind it. Night here is a tint, not a threat: the dark is a deep blue wash and no darker, the HUD keeps its own lights, and every sign, name and warning reads exactly as clearly as it did at noon. Nothing hunts better at night, and nothing hides better either. The hut has noticed, though — step onto the plaza after dark and its lanterns burn warmer against the blue, and the pale motes that drift over the meadows all day go over to fireflies, gold and patient, wherever the dark lies deepest. Banzai World keeps the same hours — the village and the meadow share the sky — but the Mall, the Arcade and the cabinets keep their own lamps and their own rules, and the Entrances hotel's dark rooms are exactly as dark as they were built to be. No more, no less.

And the regions have weather now, the way real country does — written into the land, not rolled for. The Cable Jungle gets rain: thin bright threads of it, drifting down through the green in three-hour spells. The Frozen Cache Fields get snow, slow white sparks that wander on their way down. The Kernel Badlands get the heat coming off the ground in flecks of shimmer. It turns over the way everything out there turns over — by the day and the hash, the same spell in the same place for anyone who loads the same save on the same day — and it is never, ever anything but weather: rain does not slow you, snow hides nothing, and the shimmer has never bitten anybody. The Run and the Cart look up, too: their skies take the same dawn, the same dusk, the same deep blue, with a moon standing where the sun stood — while their roads and tracks stay exactly where they were laid. Check the sky before you head out. Not because you need to. Because it is yours, and it finally looks like it.

**TWO COMMANDERS:** The war room has a second chair, and it is not a spectator's. WAR! was always a war for one pair of hands — one cursor, one camera, one purse — and a second player on the couch got a pat on the head and a tap at whatever the camera happened to be looking at. No more. Walk up to the cabinet while a battle is already burning and you do not get a map of your own: you get the war. You are a co-commander of it, with everything that means. Your own cursor, woken by your own stick and flown like the first player's — centred in your half of the screen, dead middle of your view even at the very corner of the map, where your view scrolls off the fence into the dark exactly the way theirs does. Your own selection, your own captured command bar, your own highlight: the two of you can hold the same Compiler Core at the same moment, walk your menus in opposite directions, and neither pair of hands ever knocks the other's off the table. And it is one war you are both commanding — one army, one purse of bits between you, filled by the same harvesters and spent by either chair. Train a Wrench from the second seat and the first seat watches the purse pay for it. Plant a Patch Bay from the first and the second seat's ledger drops by the same 150. Whoever launched the battle owns the campaign — the frontier advances in their name — but a victory pays both buddies their experience, because both buddies fought it.

The courtesies are worked out, too. The pause is shared and it belongs to whoever opened it: their hands drive the menu, the other commander's stick goes politely dead, and nobody resumes somebody else's pause out from under them. Leave the war mid-battle — walk out of the cabinet, any door you like — and the war does not end; the other commander simply fights on alone, the way they would have before you arrived. If the battle's owner saves and quits, the battle parks as it always has, and you are set down at your own map with your own wars exactly where you left them — a battle you were only visiting never overwrites one of yours. It works from either chair, too: the second player can launch the war and take the first player in as their guest, and everything above runs the same, mirrored. Skirmishes pull a waiting partner in the same way campaigns do. What it is not is a second front: there is one battle on the table, not two, and the hives' own purses and economies tick on exactly as they did when the war was a solo posting. Two chairs. One war. Split the screen, share the purse, and argue about the Firewall placement like professionals.

**THE REBUILDING:** Zed is down, the meadow is patched, and Patch Village has done the maths every village does the morning after: a saved world is just a world with the work left in it. So Patchina has a second list. She only shows it to a buddy who finished the first one — all ten quests, the whole story — because the second list is not errands. It is repairs. First, the cider: a barrel of it froze out on the Frozen Lake and the ice is going soft, so it is a race. Her door is the start line, the lake is the finish, and the clock rides at the top of your screen where you can watch it be unkind — and if the ice wins, she shrugs, brews more, and the race is there tomorrow. Then the long walk: DUSTER, the village's little sweep-bot, has to reach the Server Farm to scrub the racks, and its legs are small and the meadow is not, so you walk it home yourself, at its pace, the way you would walk anybody home. The hackers want nothing to do with it — it is beneath notice, which is better than armour — and it picks its way around the deep woods the way water finds a crack, so the only fight on the road is yours. And at the farm, in the racks, something has been putting itself back together out of the parts you left behind. BROKEN RAID, REASSEMBLED. It kept the receipts. It remembers your favourite move — it will hand it back to you, halved, every third exchange, the way it always did, only now it has had all this time to practise being angry about it. Beat it a second time and the village does the last repair on the list itself: PATCH DAY. Bunting between the huts, lanterns on the green, the whole village out in the light — and it is not a cutscene, it is a standing arrangement. The bunting stays up. Patchina will throw the little festival again any time you come home and ask. The gate has learned new words for the buddies who finished, too: PATCH DAY, EVERY DAY. THE VILLAGE REMEMBERS YOU. And everybody's village is their own, of course — your neighbour's Patchina has never heard of your festival, and her list is still her list. That is as it should be. A rebuilt village is a personal thing.

**THE MUSIC BREATHES:** The orchestra has learned to watch the treeline. All this time the music took its orders from a ledger — how strong you'd grown, which rung of the ladder you stood on — and it played the whole arrangement at everything: a bug ambling through somebody else's weather three fields away got the full Moonlight, bass, arpeggio and all. Now the band plays what's actually happening. Far from danger — empty country, a quiet road, nobody closing on you — the arrangement strips down to the lead alone: a handful of high notes over the quiet, the way the Moonlight sounds at 3 a.m. in an empty house. And when the field starts to mean it — a bug committed to the chase, a crowd pressing in out of the trees, a named bug off the Bounty Board stalking the party — the bass walks back in and the accompaniment fills out underneath, the whole arrangement standing up at once. It never jumps mid-phrase, either. The band changes at the bar line, the way bands do, and when a fight ends the full sound holds for one four-second breath before it lets go, so a threat flickering at the edge of the woods can't stutter the music like a nervous radio. Banzai World breathes the same way: the Sheep grazes down to a solo flute over a calm village and fills in the moment a hacker picks a fight or a hunt is on. The cabinets keep their own scores wall to wall, as they always have. The Mall keeps its full arrangement under its own lamps. And every knob in Comfort still governs the whole thing — a muted game is a silent game at every level of danger, stings included.

Speaking of stings: the Bounty Board has a voice now. When a named elite crawls out — on-screen or off, near or far — you hear two low pulses and a fall, and you know the board's bug is on the field before you've seen it. And when one goes down and the claim settles at the bank, a little triangle flourish rides in beside the till-jingle to mark the headline. The hunt announces itself coming and going now. Listen for it.
